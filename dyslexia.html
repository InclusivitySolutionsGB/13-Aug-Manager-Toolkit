What Dyslexia actually is

Dyslexia is a specific learning difference that primarily affects how the brain processes written and spoken language. It is not a problem with intelligence, vision or effort — it is a difference in how information is decoded, stored and retrieved. Beyond literacy, it can also affect working memory, processing speed, organisational thinking and the ability to hold multiple pieces of information in mind at once. Dyslexia is one of the most common neurodivergent conditions in the workplace, and one of the most commonly misread.

Dyslexia just means seeing letters backwards
Letter reversal is one small aspect. Dyslexia is fundamentally about phonological processing — how the brain maps sounds to symbols — and affects far more than visual letter recognition.
People with dyslexia aren't intelligent enough for complex roles
Dyslexia has no correlation with intelligence. Many highly successful leaders, scientists, entrepreneurs and creatives are dyslexic.
If someone can read, they can't have dyslexia
Many dyslexic people learn to read adequately — at significant cost in effort and time. The absence of obvious literacy struggle doesn't mean dyslexia isn't present.
How it might show up at work
What you might observe → what it may actually mean
Spelling errors in written work→Phonological processing works differently — not carelessness
Strong verbally, weak in writing→The key signal — capability is present, the format is the barrier
Takes longer to produce written reports→Significant additional cognitive effort — quality of thinking should be the measure
Avoids tasks involving writing→Protective response to shame and anxiety built over years
Misreads emails or instructions→Reading accuracy is effortful and affected by speed and pressure
Difficulty taking notes while listening→Dual-tasking places significant demands on working memory
Loses place in long documents→Tracking and sequencing are affected — accessible formatting helps enormously
Inconsistent performance under time pressure→Time pressure removes the compensatory strategies that usually work
What it might feel like for the individual

"Imagine being asked to write an email while someone is reading a different text out loud next to you, slightly too fast. You know exactly what you want to say. Getting it onto the page in the right order, spelled correctly, without losing your thread — that's the part that takes everything you have. Then someone points out a typo and wonders how you missed it."

For many people with dyslexia, the workplace is full of hidden effort. Reading a long document, drafting a report, proofreading before sending — tasks that feel routine to others can require significant additional time and cognitive energy. The result is often exhaustion that isn't visible to anyone else.

Shame is a significant factor. Many people with dyslexia went through school being told they were lazy, careless or not trying hard enough. Being asked to read aloud in a meeting or write on a whiteboard in front of colleagues can feel genuinely humiliating.

Inclusive practices

These are starting points, not prescriptions. Always ask the individual what would actually help them.

EasyProvide written instructions and key information in advance of meetings — this removes the pressure of capturing information in real time.
EasyAllow more time for written tasks, reports and emails — quality of thinking should be the measure, not speed of production.
EasyNever ask someone to read aloud without warning or in front of a group — this is one of the most common sources of anxiety for people with dyslexia.
EasyAccept verbal updates or recorded voice notes as an alternative to written reports where the role allows.
Some planningUse accessible document formatting — clear fonts (Arial or similar), good spacing, headings and bullet points. Avoid dense blocks of text.
Some planningAllow use of assistive technology — text-to-speech software, voice recognition tools and spell-checkers are widely available and highly effective.
Refer to specialistAccess to Work can fund dyslexia coaching, assistive technology and workplace assessments — including dyslexia specialists who can work directly with the individual.

Speaking to your team
🔒

Before speaking with the individual, don't signal to the team that a conversation is happening. Begin introducing inclusive habits as team-wide standards — this creates the right environment before any individual conversation takes place.

After agreeing adjustments, implement them in a way that doesn't draw attention to one person. Frame changes as team-wide improvements.

"I've been thinking about how we work as a team and I'm making a few changes that I think will help us all — around how we communicate, how we structure tasks and how we support each other."

"I never discuss individual team members with colleagues — that's just how I work. These changes are about how we all work together."

"Is there anything about how we communicate or work together that would help you personally?"

Supporting the wider team

A team environment that works well for neurodivergent people tends to work better for everyone. This section covers team culture shifts and guidance on supporting someone without breaching confidentiality.

📝
Make written communication accessible by defaultUse clear fonts, short paragraphs, headings and white space in team communications. Avoid walls of dense text in emails, documents and slide decks.
🎤
Value verbal contribution equallyActively create space for people to contribute verbally — in meetings, in catch-ups, in feedback processes. Written work is one measure of thinking, not the only one.
⏳
Build in time for processingShare documents ahead of meetings rather than in the meeting, and avoid creating a culture where responding instantly is seen as a virtue.
👀
Never put people on the spot with readingAsking someone to read aloud unexpectedly is uncomfortable for many people and genuinely distressing for some. Remove it as a default practice.
✅
Separate content from presentationWhen reviewing written work, focus feedback on ideas, reasoning and content — not spelling, grammar or formatting, unless the role specifically requires high written accuracy.
🔒

A team member's condition, diagnosis or any adjustments they receive are confidential. Never disclose this information to colleagues without explicit consent from the individual.

Do

  • Frame changes as team-wide communication improvements
  • Ask the individual what, if anything, they'd like colleagues to know
  • Let any disclosures be entirely on their terms and timeline
  • Challenge comments that conflate spelling with intelligence

Don't

  • Draw attention to written errors in front of others
  • Make adjustments visibly linked to one person
  • Assume colleagues will 'just understand' without disclosure
  • Underestimate someone because of how they write
